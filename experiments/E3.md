# E3 — Big-Budget Run

## Purpose

E2 found that a 15-query budget was too small to produce a trustworthy comparison: the
accuracy metric couldn't resolve the difference between strategies because a 1-3 real
edge difference rarely moves accuracy on a 36-node test set by a detectable amount (the
resolution floor is ~2.78% per single node). But E2 also found something solid underneath
that noise: `structural` consistently acquires 3-14x more real edges per query than
`random`.

E3's purpose: raise the budget enough (200 queries instead of 15) that a per-query
hit-rate advantage has room to compound into an edge-count gap large enough for accuracy
to actually detect — and, separately, start tracking real-edges-acquired as a primary
metric in its own right, not just a means to an accuracy number.

## What it runs

Script: `experiments/e3_big_budget.py`

**Parameters:**
- `SEEDS = [0, 1, 2]`
- `BUDGET = 200`
- `OBSERVE_FRAC = 0.1` — chosen deliberately: this is the setting with the most headroom
  (baseline accuracy ~0.22-0.30 vs. a ~0.69-0.75 ceiling), and it's where `structural`'s
  hit-rate advantage was originally found in E2's diagnostic.
- **Scorers tested: `random`, `structural`, `uncertainty` only.** `oracle_voi` was
  deliberately excluded — each of its queries requires a full model retrain, making a
  200-query budget far too expensive to run in the same pass; it needs its own,
  separately-scoped run (smaller budget or fewer seeds).

**Change made to `src/loop.py` for this experiment:** `run_loop` previously only tracked
`n_revealed` (total queries made, real or not) and `accuracy`. Added a third tracked
value, `n_real_edges` — the actual count of true edges present in the observed graph at
each step (`env.observed_graph().sum() / 2`) — since this is the higher-resolution metric
E2 identified as more informative than accuracy alone.

**Procedure:** for each strategy, for each seed, run the full 200-query loop, recording
both the accuracy curve and the real-edge-count curve at every step. Also compute
`full_graph_accuracy` per seed (the ceiling reference, reused from E2's methodology) for
context.

## Results

**Full-graph accuracy ceiling per seed:** `{0: 0.694, 1: 0.750, 2: 0.694}`

| Strategy | Real edges gained (→ total) | Per-seed totals | Final accuracy | AUC | AUC gap vs random |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | +6.7 → 20.7 | [23, 18, 21] | 0.343 | 62.35 | — |
| structural | +9.7 → 23.7 | [25, 23, 23] | 0.352 | 66.86 | +4.50 |
| uncertainty | +6.7 → 20.7 | [22, 20, 20] | 0.278 | 58.10 | -4.25 |

(Starting point before any querying, at `observe_frac=0.1`: ~14 real edges already
visible, accuracy roughly 0.22-0.30.)

## Interpretation

**Structural's advantage is real and more consistent than before, but smaller in relative
terms than the small-budget diagnostic suggested.** At budget=15, structural was finding
3-14x more real edges than random. At budget=200, that narrowed to roughly 1.4x (9.7
gained vs. 6.7). The likely mechanism: structural's advantage comes from a small,
finite pool of high-shared-neighbour candidates (recall — only ~5 candidates had any
structural signal at all when the observed graph was still sparse). It picks the good
ones almost immediately, then spends the bulk of the remaining ~195 queries with little
more to go on than random would have, diluting its rate advantage over the full budget.

**Structural is also noticeably more *consistent*, not just better on average.** Its
per-seed final edge counts, `[25, 23, 23]`, are tightly clustered; random's, `[23, 18,
21]`, are more spread out. This is a genuine, separate property worth keeping — a
strategy that reliably delivers a modest gain may be more useful in practice than one
with a bigger average but higher variance.

**The final-step accuracy gap is still below the metric's resolution floor.** Structural's
final accuracy (0.352) vs. random's (0.343) is a 0.9 percentage-point difference —
smaller than the ~2.78% single-node resolution floor established in E2. Only the AUC
(which aggregates the entire 200-step trajectory, not just the endpoint) shows a
consistent, non-trivial gap (+4.50) — because structural got ahead earlier in the run and
stayed ahead longer, even though where the two curves end up isn't clearly distinguishable
at the very last step.

**Uncertainty is now a clear, not just flat, negative result.** It acquired exactly as
many real edges as random (+0.0 gap) but ended up with meaningfully lower accuracy (-4.25
AUC gap). Since the quantity of real edges found was identical, this points to *which*
specific edges uncertainty tends to pick being less useful than an equal number of
randomly-picked ones — a specific, diagnosable puzzle rather than something to dismiss as
noise, though it hasn't been investigated further yet.

## Known limitations / what's not covered here

- Still only 3 seeds. An attempt was made to firm this up with 10 seeds plus a paired
  significance test (same seed gives identical starting conditions across all three
  strategies, so a paired t-test is valid and more powerful than comparing raw means) —
  that run was started but intentionally stopped and the script reverted before
  completion, to be resumed later. **The results in this document are the 3-seed numbers
  only; none of them have been confirmed as statistically significant yet.**
- `oracle_voi` has no equivalent big-budget result — it still only has the
  15-query-budget numbers from E1/E2, evaluated under conditions we now know were too
  small to trust.
- Uncertainty's specific failure mode (same edge count, worse accuracy) has been observed
  but not diagnosed — we don't yet know *why* its picks underperform equally-numerous
  random picks.
- `structural`'s diminishing relative advantage as budget grows suggests its usefulness
  may itself depend on a query budget being of the right *size*, not just the graph's
  starting density (`observe_frac`) — this interacts with, but is distinct from, the
  cold-start/ceiling tension E2 was built around, and hasn't been mapped out as its own
  sweep.
